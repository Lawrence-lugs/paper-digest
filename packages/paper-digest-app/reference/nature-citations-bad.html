Abstract

Disrupted axonal transport and astrocyte dysfunction are implicated in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Here we show these processes are linked—human-induced pluripotent stem-cell-derived astrocytes carrying the C9ORF72 mutation disrupt mitochondrial axonal transport and mitochondrial function in cocultured motor neurons (MNs). Conversely, isogenic gene-corrected astrocytes or boosting astrocytic mitochondrial bioenergetics rescue axonal mitochondrial transport deficits in C9ORF72 MNs. Our results delineate a non-cell-autonomous role for astrocytes in regulating mitochondrial transport along axons in ALS models.

Main

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive, incurable and fatal neurodegenerative condition. Mutation in the C9ORF72 gene is the commonest cause of familial ALS and accounts for ~10% of sporadic cases. Axonal transport of cargo such as mitochondria is critical for neuronal function and survival1,2, and its disruption is increasingly implicated in ALS3. Mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired motility occur in C9ORF72 MNs4,5,6, where restoring mitochondrial function rescues transport6. Astrocytes regulate neuronal metabolism and contribute to non-cell-autonomous neurodegeneration7,8, through mechanisms including reduced glutamate uptake, defective lactate shuttling and impaired mitochondrial substrate transport9,10. However, their role in regulating axonal transport remains unknown. Against this background, we used human stem-cell-derived models to address whether and how astrocytes regulate axonal mitochondrial transport in C9ORF72-ALS. We uncovered an astrocyte-mediated mechanism that controls axonal transport and found that boosting astrocytic mitochondrial metabolism is sufficient to reverse transport deficits in C9ORF72 motor neurons (MNs).

To evaluate cell-autonomous and astrocyte-derived non-cell-autonomous effects of the C9ORF72 mutation on axonal mitochondrial transport in MNs, we performed live imaging of mitochondrial transport in MNs, both in isolation and in coculture with astrocytes. We used three patient-derived human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) lines carrying the C9ORF72 mutation (C9) and their gene-corrected controls (C9Δ) generated through CRISPR–Cas9 (ref. 11), to produce highly enriched spinal cord-patterned astrocytes expressing >90% glial fibrillary acidic protein and S100B (Extended Data Fig. 1). As we previously published, notwithstanding that these astrocytes show key C9ORF72-related pathological features, such as RNA foci and dipeptide repeat proteins, they show no difference in their ability to take up extracellular glutamate and propagate calcium waves11. Separately, C9 and C9Δ iPSCs were differentiated into enriched spinal cord-patterned neuronal cultures (>90% neurons) of which 50–60% were ISL 1/2-positive MNs and devoid of astrocytes, as previously described6,12.

C9 MNs and C9Δ MNs were examined in monocultures or in coculture with the three pairs of C9 and C9Δ patient-derived astrocytes (C9-A or C9Δ-A). We measured two established features of axonal mitochondrial transport—the percentage and average velocity of motile mitochondria. First, we selectively labeled control MNs (C9Δ MNs or human embryonic stem-cell-derived HB9::EGFP (hESC HB9::EGFP MNs)) with mito-DsRed2 for measuring axonal mitochondrial transport and then cocultured them with C9-A.

As previously reported5,6, C9 MNs show a cell-autonomous axonal mitochondrial transport deficit compared to isogenic gene-corrected C9Δ MNs under monoculture conditions, establishing distinct baseline transport phenotypes. Coculturing control MNs with mutant astrocytes resulted in significant reductions in both the percentage and speed of motile mitochondria in MNs (Fig. 1a–c,e, Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 1), compared to monocultures of control MNs (Supplementary Video 2). Notably, both mitochondrial transport phenotypes were reversed when control MNs (C9Δ MNs or hESC HB9::EGFP MNs) were cocultured with control C9Δ-Α (Fig. 1a–c,e, Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 3). We then treated control MNs (hESC HB9::EGFP MNs) with conditioned medium from both C9 and C9∆ astrocytes. The absence of significant changes in mitochondrial motility (Extended Data Fig. 2b) indicates that direct contact, but not secreted factors from astrocytes, is required for the observed axonal transport deficits. Then, we examined whether cocultures of mutant MNs with mutant astrocytes would exacerbate axonal transport deficits, noting earlier reports of cell-autonomous axonal transport impairments in C9 MNs5,6. Indeed, in comparison with isolated C9 MNs (Supplementary Video 4), we observed a significant decrease in the speed of motile mitochondria when C9 MNs were cocultured with C9-A, although the percentage of motile mitochondria was not affected (Fig. 1d,f and Supplementary Video 5). Notably, coculturing C9 MNs with control C9Δ-Α rescued both axonal transport deficits (% motility and mean speed of mitochondria) to wild-type levels (Fig. 1b,d,f and Supplementary Video 6). These data recapitulate previous findings of cell-autonomous axonal mitochondrial transport deficits of C9 MNs and further indicate that, in coculture, the MN axonal transport phenotype is determined by the genotype of the astrocyte rather than that of the neurons. We subsequently assessed whether this impairment extended to signaling endosomes by tracking HcT-labeled endosomes13 in neurites of C9 and C9Δ MNs both in isolation or in coculture with C9-A or C9Δ-A. Endosomal transport in C9 MNs was unaltered and coculture with astrocytes of either genotype did not influence transport dynamics in MNs (Extended Data Fig. 2c). This indicates that the deficit is specific to mitochondria rather than a global disruption of axonal transport.

Given the established role of astrocytes in regulating brain energy homeostasis14,15, we analyzed their direct impact on mitochondrial bioenergetics in MNs using the Seahorse assay. Comparison of mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) revealed that both MN monocultures and MNs cocultured with astrocytes showed nearly eightfold higher OCR than astrocytes alone (Extended Data Fig. 3a), indicating that any significant changes in OCR values in cocultures are primarily driven by MNs. This conclusion is further supported by MitoTracker Red CMXRos assays4,16, which showed no difference in astrocytic steady-state mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) between monoculture and coculture conditions (Extended Data Fig. 3b). We corroborated previous findings that indicate impairments in both basal and maximal mitochondrial respiration in monocultures of C9 MNs with a reversal in C9Δ MNs (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c)6. Our results showed that coculturing MNs with astrocytes, irrespective of the astrocyte genotype, enhanced basal and maximal respiration compared to MN monocultures, despite comparable neuronal densities (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). Crucially, we found reductions in basal and maximal respiration when either C9 MNs or C9Δ MNs were cocultured with C9-A in comparison to coculture with C9Δ-A (Fig. 2a,b). This observation indicates that the astrocyte genotype modulates mitochondrial bioenergetics in neurons. We orthogonally validated this finding by assessing steady-state MMP in MNs using MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Fig. 2c,d). MNs were labeled using lentiviruses expressing GFP or identified using immunostaining with neuron-specific marker SMI312 postfixation. Quantification of the mean fluorescence intensity (normalized to the region of interest) of MitoTracker Red CMXRos in MNs (monocultures) showed reduced MMP in the C9 MN line compared to its paired gene-corrected control (Fig. 2c). In coculture with C9-A, the MMP of C9 MNs or C9Δ MNs was significantly reduced. Notably, this deficit was reversed in coculture with their corresponding C9Δ-A (Fig. 2d). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that astrocytes harboring a C9ORF72 mutation, upon coculture, directly mediate perturbations in the mitochondrial bioenergetics of control and mutant MNs. As astrocyte-derived lactate provides an important metabolic substrate for neuronal oxidative function7, we investigated whether the mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction in neurons was linked to altered lactate availability. To quantify intracellular lactate, C9 MNs and C9Δ MNs were transduced with a lentiviral Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based cytosolic lactate reporter (TFP donor/Venus acceptor) and cocultured with either C9-A or C9Δ-A astrocytes. No significant differences in steady-state intracellular lactate levels were detected across coculture conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4d).

Having established that astrocytes directly modulate MN mitochondrial bioenergetics, we then examined the impact of C9ORF72 mutation in isolated astrocytes on mitochondrial bioenergetics using the Seahorse assay. This revealed that C9-A, compared to their paired gene-corrected controls, displayed significantly reduced basal, adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-linked and maximal respiration (Fig. 2e). Therefore, we hypothesized that bioenergetic deficits in C9-A are responsible for their non-cell-autonomous effects on axonal mitochondrial transport in MNs. To determine whether restoring mitochondrial function in C9-A could rescue axonal transport deficits in MNs, we tested three key regulators of mitochondrial metabolism—PPARGC1A (PGC-1α), PPARGC1B (PGC-1β) and TFAM—through lentiviral overexpression. Overexpression of PGC-1β led to marked astrocyte toxicity (Extended Data Fig. 5a,k). Overexpression of PGC-1α, but not TFAM, led to upregulation of mitochondrial electron transport chain transcripts (MT-CO2, MT-ND2, MT-CO3, MT-ND4, MT-ATP6) in C9-A and was accompanied by a nonsignificant trend toward increased mitochondrial respiration (Extended Data Fig. 5b–h). These results are consistent with previous findings that TFAM overexpression does not promote mitochondrial biogenesis17. When cocultured with C9-A that overexpressed PGC-1α, both C9 MNs and C9Δ MNs showed restored mitochondrial motility and velocity (Fig. 3a–e and Supplementary Videos 7 and 8), whereas TFAM overexpression conferred no improvement (Extended Data Fig. 5i,j). In summary, we uncover that, in C9ORF72-ALS, astrocyte-specific rescue of mitochondrial bioenergetics with PGC-1α is sufficient to reverse axonal transport deficits in C9 MNs, revealing a non-cell-autonomous metabolic regulation of axonal transport by astrocytes.

This study reveals a non-cell-autonomous mechanism that regulates axonal mitochondrial transport, whereby C9 astrocytes impair the proportion and average velocity of motile mitochondria in MNs. This contrasts with the cell-autonomous deficits in C9 MNs, where the speed of motile mitochondria is unaffected5,6, suggesting distinct astrocyte-mediated mechanisms. Notably, the absence of changes in endosomal trafficking indicates that the effect is cargo specific rather than a global axonal transport defect. This selectivity likely reflects distinct energetic dependencies—signaling endosomes sustain movement through locally generated glycolytic ATP, whereas mitochondrial motility depends on membrane potential and Ca2+-regulated adaptors such as Miro, directly linking transport to the bioenergetic state of specific organelles18. Future studies should examine whether other metabolically coupled organelles, such as peroxisomes and lysosomes, show similar vulnerability.

C9 astrocytes display reduced glycolytic capacity, lactate production (Extended Data Fig. 6) and mitochondrial respiration, indicating bioenergetic deficiency. RNA sequencing revealed no genotype-driven astrocytic reactivity (Extended Data Fig. 7), making a gain-of-toxic-function mechanism unlikely. In coculture, C9-A lowered neuronal basal and maximal respiration, indicating a direct influence on neuronal mitochondrial bioenergetics. Steady-state intraneuronal lactate levels did not vary significantly across coculture conditions, suggesting that disrupted oxidative metabolism in astrocytes, rather than altered lactate transfer, mediates the deficit. Restoring astrocytic bioenergetics through PGC-1α overexpression19,20 enhanced astrocytic respiration and rescued neuronal mitochondrial motility, defining a causal link between astrocytic mitochondrial metabolism and neuronal mitochondrial dynamics. These findings align with studies in SOD1 mice, where PGC-1α activation preserved mitochondrial function and delayed disease progression21. A deeper understanding of the mechanistic basis by which astrocytes influence axonal mitochondrial transport is important and requires further study. This includes evaluating direct mitochondrial exchange, transfer of metabolites and lipids, vesicular signaling and broader metabolic coupling processes that coordinate astrocytic and neuronal energy homeostasis.

In summary, these results define a mitochondria-specific, bioenergetically coupled mechanism by which astrocytes regulate axonal transport. Restoring astrocytic bioenergetics through activation of the PGC-1α pathway represents a potential therapeutic strategy to correct non-cell-autonomous mitochondrial dysfunction in C9ORF72-ALS. Future studies should delineate the molecular mediators linking astrocytic energy metabolism to neuronal mitochondrial signaling and determine whether similar mechanisms operate across other ALS genotypes and sporadic disease.

Methods

Generation of hiPSC lines and isogenic gene-corrected control hiPSC lines

Dermal fibroblasts from three C9ORF72-ALS/FTD patients (C9-1, C9-2, C9-3) harboring the G4C2 repeat expansion in the C9ORF72 gene were acquired and reprogrammed under approval from the University of Edinburgh Research Ethics Committee. Details of the C9 hiPSC lines, including clinical metadata of the patients, are listed in ref. 6. The three C9ORF72 donors comprised one female (C9-1) and two males (C9-2 and C9-3). All donors provided informed consent for the use of their samples in research. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. Gene-edited controls were generated through CRISPR–Cas9 technology as reported6,11,12. Over the course of this study, karyotypes of all hiPSC lines were examined using the conventional Giemsa banding chromosomal analysis, conducted by The Doctors Laboratory. The exclusion of mycoplasma contamination was ascertained through monthly testing of hiPSC culture supernatants using the VenorGeM Classic detection kit (Minerva Biolabs GmbH). hiPSC lines were cultured with Essential 8 Medium and passaged using Dispase/Collagenase.

hiPSC MN generation

Spinal MN differentiation from parental and gene-edited hiPSCs (one C9-mutant/gene-corrected MN pair was used throughout the study) was performed using an established protocol with minor modifications, yielding a highly enriched and electrophysiologically mature neuronal culture, devoid of glia, with approximately 60% of cells being positive for islet-1 and islet-2 homeobox MN markers 1 week postplating6. hiPSCs were dissociated into single cells using 1X Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich) and neuralized as a suspension culture using dual-SMAD inhibition with SB-431542 (20 μM; Tocris Bio-Techne), LDN-193189 (0.1 μM; Selleckchem) and potentiation with the Wnt-agonist, CHIR-99021 (3 μM; Tocris Bio-Techne) in N2/B27 medium, which consists of 0.5× Neurobasal (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5× Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× Antibiotic–Antimycotic (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× GlutaMAX (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× B-27 supplement (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× N-2 supplement (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 10 μM L-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich). On day 2, neural spheres were simultaneously patterned to spinal cord identity by treatment with retinoic acid (RA; 0.1 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) and smoothened agonist (0.5 μM; Sigma-Aldrich), promoting caudalization and ventralization, respectively, along with SB-431542, LDN-193189 and CHIR-99021 in N2/B27 medium for an additional 5 days. On day 7, spheres were maintained in culture with RA and Smoothened agonist, supplemented with recombinant human brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF; 10 ng ml−1; R&D Systems) in N2/B27 medium to generate MN progenitors. From day 9, MN progenitors were cultured in the day 7 medium with the addition of DAPT (10 μM; Tocris Bio-Techne), an inhibitor of Notch signaling, for an additional 5–7 days. At days 14–16, MN spheres were dissociated using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cells were plated as a monolayer onto dishes coated with laminin from Engelbreth–Holm–Swarm mouse sarcoma basement membrane (5 μg ml−1; Sigma-Aldrich), fibronectin human plasma (10 μg ml−1; Sigma-Aldrich) and Matrigel (1:20), which had been pretreated with poly-L-ornithine bromide (100 µg ml−1; Sigma-Aldrich). On day 0, the cells were cultured in MN neurotrophic factor (MN-NF) medium, which contains 1× Neurobasal, 1× Antibiotic–Antimycotic, 1× GlutaMAX, 1× MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol, 1× B-27 supplement, 1× N-2 supplement, RA (1 μM), ascorbic acid (2.5 μM), BDNF (10 ng ml−1), recombinant human glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (10 ng ml−1; R&D Systems), recombinant human ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF; 10 ng ml−1; R&D Systems) and animal-free recombinant human insulin-like growth factor-1 (10 ng ml−1; PeproTech), along with 25 μM uridine/5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (U/FDU). From day 2 onward, the medium was regularly changed every 2–3 days and supplemented with U/FDU (1 μM; Sigma-Aldrich) for at least 1-week postplating, to eliminate residual proliferating cells. For MNs, independent biological replicates/plate downs refer to distinct neuralization and differentiation steps originating from the hiPSC state.

hiPSC astrocyte generation

Astrocytes were generated from hiPSCs using established lab protocols and were characterized through expression of astrocyte-specific markers and implementation of functional assays (glutamate uptake assay and calcium imaging) as reported in earlier publications11. Specifically, hiPSCs were neuralized and subsequently converted into spheres following the established protocols detailed above. The spheres were then cultured in MN maturation medium for a duration of 2–4 weeks. After this maturation phase, the spheres were chopped and cultured in NSCR EL20 medium for an additional 4–6 weeks to induce astrogliogenesis. At the conclusion of this conversion phase, the medium was switched to NSCR EF20 medium to support the proliferation of astrocyte progenitor cells (APCs) within the spheres. These astrospheres were mechanically chopped every 4 weeks to ensure good viability and were maintained long term in EF20 medium (>4 chops). The resulting astrospheres were dissociated into single cells using the Papain Dissociation System (Worthington Biochemical) and plated onto six-well plates coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, diluted 1:80) at a density of 7.5 × 105 cells per well. These cells were subsequently differentiated into astrocytes by transitioning the medium to AstroMed CNTF medium for a period of 2 weeks. Throughout the astrocyte generation process, the media were changed every 2–3 days to ensure optimal growth conditions. The NSCR EF20 medium comprised Advanced DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen), 1% N2 supplement (Invitrogen), 0.1% B-27 supplement (Invitrogen), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen), 1% GlutaMAX solution (Invitrogen), 20 ng ml−1 fibroblast growth factor 2 (PeproTech) and 20 ng ml−1 epidermal growth factor (R&D Systems). The AstroMed CNTF medium consisted of Neurobasal medium (Invitrogen), 0.2% B-27 supplement (Invitrogen), 1% nonessential amino acids (Invitrogen), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen), 1% GlutaMAX (Invitrogen) and 10 ng ml−1 CNTF (R&D Systems).

We used three patient-derived iPSC astrocyte lines carrying the C9ORF72 mutation (C9), along with their isogenic gene-corrected control with the C9ORF72 mutation removed (C9∆) using CRISPR–Cas9. For each hiPSC line, APCs were derived and banked from two separate dissociations of astrospheres. For each biological experiment/plate down, APCs were thawed out and differentiated independently into astrocytes over 2 weeks.

Cocultures of MNs and astrocytes

APCs were dissociated into single cells and plated in astrocyte differentiation medium (AstroMed CNTF medium) to differentiate into mature astrocytes over 2 weeks, as described above. At 2 weeks, MN spheres (day 16) were dissociated into single cells using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA and plated on astrocyte monolayers in MN-NF medium (1× Neurobasal, 1× Antibiotic–Antimycotic, 1× GlutaMAX, 1× MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol, 1× B-27 supplement, 1× N-2 supplement, RA (1 μM), ascorbic acid (2.5 μM), recombinant human BDNF (10 ng ml−1, 248-BD/CF, R&D Systems), recombinant human glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (10 ng ml−1; 212-GD, R&D Systems), recombinant CNTF (10 ng ml−1; 257-NT, R&D Systems) and animal-free recombinant human insulin-like growth factor-1 (10 ng ml−1; AF-100–11, PeproTech).

Astrocyte–MN cocultures and MN monocultures were maintained for 3 weeks in MN-NF medium before performing experiments. MN-NF medium was changed every 2–3 days and supplemented with U/FDU (1 μM; U3003 and F0503, Sigma-Aldrich) for at least 1 week after plate down to remove residual proliferating cells. Cell densities used for coculture experiments are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Axonal transport studies

Astrocyte–MN cocultures were generated on μ-Slide eight-well plates as described above. MN monocultures were also produced by plating MNs as a monolayer on µ-Slide eight-well ibidi dishes as explained in prior work6,22. At plate down, the MNs were sparsely transduced with lentivirus expressing mito-DsRed2 as follows:

  1. 1.

    MN monocultures—multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.5–1, adjusted to obtain 1–2 labeled cells per field of view6.

  2. 2.

    Astrocyte–MN cocultures—mito-DsRed2 lentivirus was added at MOI 10 to approximately 10,000 MNs (per well; on μ-slide eight-well plates) and MNs were selectively transduced before being transferred onto fully differentiated astrocytes.

Live imaging of mitochondrial axonal transport was conducted at 21 days post-MN plate down at ×63 magnification (Plan-Apochromat 1.40 NA oil DIC M27 objective, Carl Zeiss) using an Axio Observer Z1 inverted motorized microscope (Carl Zeiss). The microscope was equipped with a Cy3 FL filter set (Carl Zeiss), Zen 2011 z stack, time lapse and Definite Focus modules (Carl Zeiss) and an S1 Environmental System (Carl Zeiss) incubation chamber for temperature control at 37 °C and 5% CO2 flow. Time-lapse imaging of mitochondria in axons was conducted as follows6,22: for all conditions, measurements were acquired from the proximal portion of the axon and the axonal segments selected for recording were at least 100 μm from the cell soma to avoid the axon hillock—the part of the axon at the juncture with the cell body. Images were acquired at a capture rate of 0.2 Hz for 5 min, covering a ~100 µm stretch of axon and a small z stack, if required. Motile mitochondria were defined as those traveling at or above the cutoff speed of 0.1 µm s−1 (ref. 5). Time-lapse images were used to generate videos, exported from the Zeiss software and further analysis was implemented in Fiji. Kymographs were generated and analyzed using KymoToolBox ImageJ plugin17 to determine the numbers of static (≤0.1 µm s−1) compared to bidirectionally motile mitochondria (either toward or away from the soma). A minimum of four axons (n) were imaged per condition per biological replicate (n, where n = 3). The percentage of motile mitochondria (labeled with mito-DsRed2) relative to the total number of mitochondria was quantified in a 100 µm stretch of axon in C9ORF72 and gene-edited MNs. For calculating the mean speed of motile mitochondria in each segment measured, only mitochondria with a speed higher than 0.1 μm s−1 over the recorded section of time (5 min) were included in the analysis. The average speed of those mitochondria per axon was taken forward.

Metabolic profiling of astrocytes and neurons (Seahorse assay)

The following cell densities were used for the Seahorse assay:

  1. 1.

    Astrocyte monocultures—APCs were plated on V7 Seahorse plates (100777-004, Agilent) at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well to ascertain a highly confluent cellular monolayer. They were cultured in CNTF medium for differentiation into astrocytes over 2 weeks.

  2. 2.

    MN monocultures—MNs were plated on poly-L-ornithine bromide (100 µg ml−1; P3655, Sigma-Aldrich)-treated V28 Seahorse plates (100882-004, Agilent). The plates were subsequently coated overnight as described in ref. 6. Neuronal density was 50,000 cells per well.

  3. 3.

    Astrocyte–MN cocultures—APCs were dissociated into single cells and plated at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well on V28 Seahorse plates in CNTF to differentiate progenitors into mature astrocytes over 2 weeks. At 2 weeks, MN spheres (day 16) were dissociated into single cells using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA and plated on astrocyte monolayers in MN-NF medium at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well.

The standard Agilent Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress protocol was modified depending on the condition and the experiments were conducted at 21 days post-MN plate down using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent).

Mitochondrial function

The Seahorse reader can measure OCR, an excellent readout for mitochondrial respiratory activity and the current choice of experiment to examine underlying mitochondrial dysfunction. Culture medium was exchanged for low-buffering capacity Seahorse XF base medium (102353, Agilent) supplemented with 1× GlutaMAX, 10 mM glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) and 2 mM pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), adjusted to pH 7.35 ± 0.5 at 37 °C with 1 M sodium hydroxide. The cells were equilibrated for 30 min at 37 °C with no CO2, before being inserted into the machine. OCR was measured basally and after sequential addition of specific mitochondrial inhibitors, as illustrated in Supplementary Table 2. FCCP dose was optimized for astrocyte and neuronal monocultures (tested doses for neurons = 0.125 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM; tested doses for astrocytes = 0.125 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM) and the concentration adopted was the one that maximized the OCR response. The protocol involved a 3-min mixing, 2-min wait and 3-min measurement cycle (total duration of each cycle was 8 min). Three measurements/cycles were obtained basally (before drug injections; total duration of 24 min) and three measurements were taken after the addition of each drug (first, oligomycin; second, FCCP; third, antimycin/rotenone; fourth, 2-deoxy-D-glucose).

Glycolytic rate measurement

A modified version of the Seahorse Glycolytic Stress Test protocol was used. hiPSC-derived astrocyte monocultures, differentiated and plated as above, were washed and incubated in Seahorse Assay Medium supplemented with 1 mM glutamine and with zero glucose for 30 min at 37 °C with no CO2. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured using the Seahorse pH probe basally and after sequential addition of specific metabolic modifiers at the doses in Supplementary Table 3 (first, glucose; second, oligomycin; third, 2-deoxy-D-glucose), with intermittent mixing and measurement cycles as described above.

Five individual wells were used per condition in each plate, with experiments repeated in three independent plate downs from different MN differentiations (n = 3). In astrocyte and MN monocultures, the measured OCR ((O2)/time) and ECAR were normalized to total protein content using the bicinchoninic acid assay (23225, Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific)16. For astrocyte-neuronal cocultures, neuronal counts were estimated through neuron-specific staining (immunocytochemistry with SMI312 at 1:1,000 (837904, BioLegend)). Data were visualized in and imported from Seahorse Wave Desktop software (version 2.6.0.31; Agilent). ‘Basal respiration’, which represents the net sum of all processes in the cell capable of consuming O2, including mitochondria and other oxidases, was calculated by subtracting nonmitochondrial respiration (that is, the minimum OCR value after antimycin/rotenone addition) from the OCR value right before oligomycin injection. ‘Maximal respiration’ was calculated by subtracting nonmitochondrial respiration from the highest OCR measurement after FCCP injection. ‘ATP-linked respiration’ was calculated by subtracting the oligomycin rate from baseline cellular OCR. ‘Glycolytic capacity’ was calculated by subtracting baseline nonglycolic acidification from the maximum ECAR rate (ECAR differential) achieved after the addition of oligomycin, which stops oxidative phosphorylation and maximizes cell glycolysis for energy provision. Baseline nonglycolytic acidification was determined after the addition of 2-deoxyglucose and, where indicated, antimycin/rotenone, and this value was subtracted from total ECAR to isolate glycolysis-dependent acidification. If negative values were obtained, they were excluded from the analysis, as they are nonphysiological.

FRET metabolic imaging of astrocytes

hiPSC-derived astrocytes were transfected with plasmids encoding FRET probes for glucose (FLII12Pglu-700uDelta6; 17866, Addgene)23 or lactate (Laconic; 44238, Addgene)23. This was done 24 h before metabolic measurement using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies) at 0.65 μg of DNA per well and 2.33 μl Lipofectamine 2000 per well for 45 min23. Metabolic imaging was performed with astrocytes being continuously perfused at 37 °C with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (composition—150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES buffer, 0.1 mM glycine, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM glucose, pH 7.4). Images were captured using a DFC350 FX digital camera as part of a Leica AF6000 LX imaging system. Images were acquired every 10 s. All probes were imaged with a standard FRET CFP/YFP filter wheel, with excitation of CFP and measurements taken within the CFP and YFP emission spectra. For glucose measurement, the YFP:CFP ratio of FLII12Pglu-700uDelta6 FRET probe was used to determine intracellular glucose concentrations for individual astrocytes. Levels were measured at baseline before the addition of the glucose-uptake inhibitor cytochalasin B (20 μM) after 60 s. This resulted in a reduction in intracellular glucose levels corresponding to the rate of glucose consumption. For lactate measurements, the CFP:YFP ratio of the Laconic FRET probe was used to determine intracellular lactate levels. Levels were measured at baseline in artificial cerebrospinal fluid, before the addition of the MCT inhibitor AR-C155858 (1 μM). This resulted in an increase in concentration corresponding to intracellular lactate production. All FRET ratios were normalized by subtracting baseline and expressed as percentage of maximum. A linear least-squares fitting routine was used to determine the line of best fit, and slopes were calculated for the portion of the curve corresponding to the rate of consumption or production of substrate.

FRET-based assessment of intracellular neuronal lactate

Intracellular neuronal lactate was quantified with the genetically encoded FRET biosensor Laconic, delivered to MNs by lentiviral transduction (1:75; LV-cppt-CMV-Laconic-opre, pLenti6 backbone). The Laconic construct used for neuronal transduction was derived from the same plasmid (44238, Addgene) used for astrocyte transduction. The three iPSC-derived astrocyte lines and their gene-corrected pairs were differentiated for 2 weeks; dissociated MNs were transduced and plated onto confluent astrocyte monolayers and maintained for 21 days in MN-NF (fed on alternate days). For imaging, cultures were transferred 24 h beforehand into phenol red-free MN-NF and supplemented with 20 mM HEPES immediately before acquisition. Live imaging was performed at 37 °C on a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan (×10 objective; 2,000 × 2,000 px). The donor (mTFP) was excited at 430–460 nm, and emission was collected every 20 s in five channels—ch1 donor emission (mTFP, 470–500 nm) under donor excitation; ch2 acceptor emission (Venus, 520–560 nm) under donor excitation; ch3 direct mTFP excitation/emission; ch4 direct Venus excitation/emission; and ch5 brightfield. Lactate binding to Laconic reduces FRET efficiency, increasing donor (mTFP) and decreasing acceptor (Venus) fluorescence; accordingly, the mTFP/Venus emission ratio increases with intracellular lactate and was used as the readout in MN somata. To validate sensor performance under our conditions, control MN monocultures were imaged at baseline and after exogenous lactate; the mTFP:Venus ratio increased by ~5.78% (ΔR/R0), consistent with prior Laconic use23. For cocultures, the four combinations (C9 MN/C9-A, C9Δ MN/C9-A, C9 MN/C9Δ-A and C9Δ MN/C9Δ-A) were analyzed across three independent plate downs; per plate down, 2–5 coverslips per condition were imaged (2 regions per coverslip; ≥5 MN somata per field).

Mitochondrial staining for determination of neuronal MMP

Astrocyte–MN cocultures were generated on 96-well plates as described above. MN monocultures were also produced by plating MNs on 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well. Neurons were labeled and demarcated from astrocytes either at plate down with transduction with lentivirus expressing GFP or immunostained after fixation with neuron-specific marker SMI312 at 1:1,000 (837904, BioLegend). After 21 days of MN plate down, astrocyte–MN cocultures and MN monocultures were incubated with 100 nM of potentiometric dye MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 45 min at 37 °C4. This is a membrane-permeant, lipophilic, cationic fluorescence probe that is sequestered in the mitochondrial membrane of living cells, and its accumulation is dependent on mitochondrial potential16. The covalent binding of the dye to the polarized inner mitochondrial membrane allows its retention during washing and fixation of cells and makes it suitable for fluorescence microscopy. The cells were then washed with PBS and fixed in 4% PFA. Fluorescence was visualized using Opera Phenix Plus high-content screening system at ×40 magnification (OperaPHX/OPRTCLS water immersion objective x40: NA 1.1, WD 0.62 mm (field of view: approximately 323 × 323 μm). Flat-field correction was automatically performed by Harmony Software during image acquisition. Harmony or Signals Image Artist was used for image analysis.

Mitochondrial staining for determination of astrocyte MMP

Astrocyte–MN cocultures and astrocyte monocultures were generated on 96-well plates as described above. hiPSC-derived astrocytes were transduced with BFP lentivirus at plate down at a MOI of 25 to demarcate from neurons. APCs were first differentiated into astrocytes in monoculture for 14 days. MNs were then plated onto a subset of astrocyte cultures to generate cocultures and were maintained for a further 21 days. To enable direct comparison, astrocyte monocultures were maintained in parallel for the same overall duration (14 days differentiation + additional 21 days). At the end of the culture period, both astrocyte monocultures and astrocyte–MN cocultures were incubated with 100 nM of potentiometric dye MitoTracker Red CMXRos for 45 min. The cells were then washed with PBS and fixed in 4% PFA. Immunostaining was used to demarcate cell types with neuron-specific marker SMI312 and astrocyte-specific marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (ab4674, Abcam). Fluorescence was visualized using Opera Phenix Plus high-content screening system at ×40 magnification (OperaPHX/OPRTCLS water immersion objective ×40: NA 1.1, WD 0.62 mm (field of view—approximately 323 × 323 μm)). Flat-field correction was automatically performed by Harmony Software during image acquisition. Harmony was used for image analysis. Integrated fluorescence intensity of CMXRos in BFP+ cytoplasmic cell body area was calculated.

Lentiviral overexpression of PGC-1α

Previously generated PGC-1α overexpression lentivirus6 was transduced into C9 APCs at plating at a MOI of 10 and differentiated into astrocytes over 2 weeks.

Lentiviral overexpression of PGC-1β and TFAM

APCs were transduced at the time of plating with lentiviral constructs encoding PGC-1β (Lv-cppt-CMV2-PGC1B-P2A-moeGFP-opre) or TFAM (Lv-cppt-CMV2-TFAM-P2A-moeGFP-opre), both sharing the same backbone as the PGC-1α vector to allow coexpression with GFP for verification of transduction. Cells were subsequently differentiated into astrocytes over 2 weeks. PGC-1β overexpression at a MOI of 5–10 resulted in astrocytic toxicity, whereas TFAM overexpression at MOI 10 was well-tolerated. Expression was confirmed by GFP fluorescence, showing uniform transduction across astrocyte cultures (Extended Data Fig. 5a). GFP-positive TFAM-expressing astrocytes were subsequently cocultured with mito-DsRed2-labeled MNs for live imaging and functional analysis (Extended Data Fig. 5a).

Real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR of mitochondrially encoded transcripts in astrocytes

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini kit (74104, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA was removed using the on-column DNase method (79254, Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized using 250 ng of RNA with the DyNAmo cDNA Synthesis Kit (10748908, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Technical replicates, as well as no template and no RT negative controls, were included, and at least three biological replicates were studied in each case. Real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR reactions were set up with DyNAmo ColorFlash SYBR Green qPCR kit (10442308, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and run on a CFX96 System (Bio-Rad). The data were visualized and exported using Maestro 1.1 software (Bio-Rad). Primers were synthesized by Sigma-Aldrich. Primer sequences and annealing temperatures used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Live imaging of endosomal transport

Signaling endosomes were visualized by live imaging of the fluorescence of the atoxic tetanus neurotoxin binding fragment (HcT)13. MNs (C9 and C9Δ) were cultured alone or in coculture with astrocytes across the three independent iPSC-derived patient lines with their corresponding gene-corrected controls for 21 days in vitro. Imaging was performed on a Leica FALCON confocal microscope equipped with a white-light laser, HyD-X detector and ×60/1.4 NA oil-immersion objective, within a temperature-controlled and CO2-controlled (37 °C, 5% CO2) chamber. Time-lapse sequences were acquired at 0.2 Hz (1 frame every 5 s) for 5 min (~60 frames).

For each condition, five to seven neurites were analyzed, tracking 1–15 motile endosomes per neurite. Endosomes between 200 nm and 1 µm in diameter were prioritized, with larger (2–3 µm) endosomes included when clearly motile. Cargoes were defined as motile if their displacement exceeded 0.1 µm s−1 for at least ten consecutive frames; tracking was terminated after >10 stationary frames (terminal pausing).

Kymographs were generated in ImageJ, and velocities were extracted from displacement–time slopes. Both anterograde and retrograde trajectories were quantified, and movement was described as neuritic transport given that axons and dendrites could not be unequivocally distinguished in these mature mass cultures. Mean endosomal velocities were averaged per neurite.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis for Figs. 1–3 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 4–6 was performed using R (version 4.3.2)24. Given the complex hierarchical structure of the data, outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models that were fitted using the ‘lme4’ package25. Individual iPSC lines, differentiation batch, axon, well and imaging field were modeled as random effects where appropriate. Fixed effects comprised astrocyte genotype, MN genotype, metabolic manipulation (PGC-1α overexpression) or combinations thereof, depending on the experimental design. This approach is appropriate for non-normally distributed data and explicitly accounts for within-sample dependencies, thereby avoiding pseudoreplication26. We compared individual mutant iPSC lines to their corresponding gene-corrected isogenic controls, as illustrated in Figs. 1–3 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 4–6. Fixed effects were assessed with Wald chi-square tests using the ‘car’ R package27, and, where appropriate, Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were performed on estimated marginal means with Wald tests using the ‘emmeans’ R package28, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

No data were excluded from the analyses unless predefined technical quality criteria were not met. For instance, Seahorse glycolytic analyses and nonphysiological negative ECAR values were excluded as described above.

Statistical analysis for Extended Data Figs. 1 and 3 was performed using Prism (version 8.4.0; GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. Data were initially determined to be parametric or nonparametric before applying the appropriate statistical analysis, with correction for multiple comparisons, as stated in the legends (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). Throughout the study, independent biological replicates are defined as independently performed experiments. RNA-sequencing data presented in Extended Data Fig. 7 were analyzed using DESeq2.

Sample sizes were selected based on previous studies using comparable hiPSC-derived MN and astrocyte models and on prior experience with the experimental assays used in this study. Experimental groups were defined by genotype and coculture condition and were therefore not randomized. Details of biological and technical replicates are provided in the corresponding Methods and figure legends. Quantitative image analyses were performed blindly to experimental condition.
