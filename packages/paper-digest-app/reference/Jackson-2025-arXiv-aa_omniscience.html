AA-Omniscience: Evaluating Cross-Domain Knowledge Reliability in Large Language Models

Declan Jackson, William Keating, George Cameron, Micah Hill-Smith · Artificial Analysis

Verdict. A benchmark paper whose useful contribution is operational: it scores short-form factual recall with an abstention-aware index (OI) over 6,000 source-grounded, GPT-5-generated questions in 42 topics. OI is essentially the penalty-weighted average score from earlier short-form factuality work, fixed at penalty 1, so the headline (Claude 4.1 Opus at 4.8, only three of 36 models above 0) rests on that penalty choice, on an LLM generator and grader whose agreement with humans is not quantified, and on a prompt that tells models to abstain. Treat it as a diagnostic of selective answering, not as a reliability ranking. overall confidence 70%

Motivation80%

  • Capability benchmarks (coding, MMLU-style understanding, tool use) score correctness, not hallucination or knowing when not to answer, so they do not help choose a model for knowledge-heavy work.
  • Accuracy-only scoring rewards guessing. The authors lean on Kalai et al. (2025) to argue this reinforces overconfidence during post-training.
  • Embedded knowledge still matters with tools: a model needs context and acronyms to search well, and a calibrated model can route to tools only when needed (one chat example, Fig. 2).
  • Aggregated factuality benchmarks hide per-domain variation, so model choice by use case needs domain-level results.

My read. The first two points are credible and well argued. The third is asserted with one anecdote and no experiment. The domain choice is justified by the six domains covering 44% of 2024 U.S. wages, which supports the importance of the topics but says little about how representative the questions are.

Core idea85%

Idea 1: the Omniscience Index. Let \(c,p,i,a\) be the counts of correct, partially correct, incorrect and abstained answers, and \(N=c+p+i+a\). The paper defines

\[ \mathrm{OI}=100\cdot\frac{c-i}{c+p+i+a},\qquad \mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{c}{c+p+i+a},\qquad \mathrm{Hallucination\ rate}=\frac{i}{p+i+a}. \]

Two consequences the paper does not spell out. First, OI is accuracy minus the share of questions answered wrongly:

\[ \mathrm{OI}=100\left(\mathrm{Accuracy}-\frac{i}{N}\right). \]

Second, it is one point of a family of penalty-weighted scores

\[ S_\lambda=\frac{c-\lambda\, i}{N}, \]

with \(\lambda=1\) (the paper calls this penalty \(p\), which collides with its partial-answer count \(p\)). At \(\lambda=1\), answering pays off only above 50% confidence, partial answers and abstentions both score 0, and a model that abstains on everything scores exactly 0.

Idea 2: a source-grounded question agent. A GPT-5-based agent derives questions from authoritative, up-to-date documents, then filters them (similarity, difficulty, ambiguity, source reliance) and revises them (Table 1 of the paper) toward single, short, exact answers. Difficulty is checked against frontier models from many labs. Evaluation gives models no tools or context, with a system prompt saying it is better to say you do not know. A grader, Gemini 2.5 Flash Preview (09-2025) with reasoning, labels each answer correct, partially correct, incorrect or not attempted.

There is no framework figure; the paper is a benchmark and metric paper.

Distinguishing factor70%

  • Short-form factuality (SimpleQA, Wei et al. 2024a). Same format: one short gold answer, an LLM grader, correct / incorrect / not attempted. That work already discusses an average score with a penalty for wrong answers and correct-given-attempted and F-score metrics. AA-Omniscience fixes the penalty at 1, rescales to [-100, 100], and extends coverage to 42 topics in six domains with per-domain reporting.
  • Human-annotated factuality sets (FreshQA, FActScore, Natural Questions). Replaced by an agent that generates, filters and revises questions, with unspecified manual review. The claimed gain is scalability and continual updating.
  • Long-form factuality (Wei et al. 2024b). Builds on its automated question generation, but feeds source documents into the generation prompt instead of few-shot GPT-4 prompts.
  • Hallucination benchmarks (HalluLens). Per the authors, they measure fidelity to input or training data, while this measures recall of facts from authoritative sources.
  • Single-domain benchmarks (LawBench, GPQA). This one reports across domains.
  • Authors' earlier version. None in the text. Results are a snapshot of a leaderboard the authors say is continuously updated.

My read. The closest ancestor is SimpleQA. The novelty is breadth, the generation pipeline, and a default parameter choice. The paper never compares OI with earlier metrics on the same models, so it cannot show what OI reveals that they miss.

Paper’s conclusion. Claude 4.1 Opus has the highest OI (4.8), only three models score above zero, which the authors read as persistent factuality and calibration weaknesses; leaders differ by domain, so models should be chosen by use case.

Supported? Partly. That most models answer wrongly more than rightly on these questions is supported, though inflated by adversarial filtering. The first-place claim holds only to within a few points. “Calibration” is overstated, since no confidence is elicited or scored (see ICYMI). Domain leadership lacks intervals. “Choose by domain” is sensible advice but not established by these margins.

In case you missed it70%

−The headline explanation is an identity. Because \(\mathrm{OI}=100(\mathrm{Accuracy}-i/N)\), the statement that high hallucination is the dominant factor driving low scores follows from the metric, not from the data.
−Calibration is claimed but not measured. No confidence is elicited and no calibration statistic (such as expected calibration error) is reported. OI scores binary answer-or-abstain behavior under a prompt that asks for abstention, so it mixes knowledge, calibration and instruction-following. There is no run without the abstention instruction.
−The penalty is a free parameter and the prompt does not state it. The \(\lambda=1\) choice has no sensitivity analysis, and the answerer prompt only says it is better to say you do not know. As I recall, Kalai et al. recommend stating explicit confidence thresholds in the instructions; I did not re-check that here. The authors themselves float a −0.5 penalty. My rough arithmetic from the rounded figures: at \(\lambda=0.5\), Opus is about 20.4, Grok 4 about 19.5, GPT-5 (high) about 14.3, so the top two are nearly tied.
−Precision is graded asymmetrically. Appendix A.2 grades “1.5” against gold “1.5.0” as INCORRECT (Example 3), while “28.1 million” against gold “28” is only PARTIAL (Example 5). Under the penalty, too little detail costs a point and too much detail is free, which may disproportionately hit version- and number-heavy topics such as software and science.
−Generator and filter circularity, acknowledged but untested. GPT-5 writes, filters and revises the questions, and GPT-5 variants are among the ranked models. Questions are also screened against frontier models that the paper does not name. The authors list the first as a limitation but report no check of it.
−Partial answers are neither rewarded nor penalized. They score 0 in OI, the same as abstaining, yet they sit in the hallucination-rate denominator. A model that gives coarse-but-right answers is treated like one that declines.
+Honest handling of the abstain-everything degenerate case. The authors note that never answering scores 0, which would rank 4th of 36 (so 33 models are at or below it), report the lowest observed attempt rate (42%, next lowest 65%), and propose a gentler penalty.
·Only 10% of questions are public. About 600 questions are released, stratified to the full distribution, to limit contamination. That protects the benchmark but means third parties cannot reproduce or audit the full-set numbers.
·Questions carry a “first answerable date.” The pipeline records when the needed information first became public and favors recent facts. Fewer than 2% of questions date from 2025, so the authors consider the penalty for earlier-cutoff models minimal.

Cautious judgement65%

QuestionAnswerConf.
Fair comparison to external baselines?Partly. Inside the benchmark the comparison is even: one prompt, no tools, one grader, one pass. But there is no external baseline: OI is not compared with SimpleQA-style metrics on the same models, the only comparator is the authors’ own Intelligence Index, and the filter models and human-grading agreement are not disclosed.60%
Invents its own FoM?Yes, nominally. OI is a named new index, but it is the known penalty-weighted average score at penalty 1, rescaled to [-100, 100]. Accuracy and hallucination rate are simple ratios, and the Intelligence Index comparator is also in-house.85%
Rewrite of an existing idea?Mostly a recombination. Metric from SimpleQA-style scoring, motivation from Kalai et al. (2025), pipeline as an automated, source-grounded version of human-curated sets. New: 42-topic, six-domain coverage with per-domain reporting, generator-at-scale, and the model comparison.75%
Reliable enough to reuse?Reuse the metric and prompts as a diagnostic of selective answering. Do not rely on rank order among the top few models or on domain winners without intervals. The full set is private, so audit is limited. If you reuse it: bootstrap intervals over questions, sweep the penalty, and hand-check the grader on a sample.65%
